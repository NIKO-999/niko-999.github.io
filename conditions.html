Rhythm

The flattest light in the country

// input: photoperiod at 35.7°S · annual swing 4 h 50 m · born 44 days before the trough

Whangārei is the mildest light regime New Zealand has — a four-hour-fifty swing between midsummer and midwinter, against seven hours in Invercargill. They call Northland the winterless north and mean it. You grew up with seasons soft enough to ignore, so you ignore them. You plan the year as a flat line, and you're nearly right — which is exactly why the dip, when it lands, gets read as a personal failure instead of as late June.

And note the direction you arrived in: born six weeks before the shortest day, your first half-year ran a brief slide into the dark, then five straight months of climb. The first trend your nervous system ever tracked was improving light.

Phase

A life lived behind the sun

// input: 174.32°E vs the 180° meridian · solar noon ≈ 12:19–12:26 pm · +1 h in dst months

Every New Zealander lives behind the sun — the whole country sits west of its own time meridian, so solar noon lands after clock noon for every person in it. In Whangārei the gap is about 23 minutes; add daylight saving and your true midday spends half the year landing near 1:20 pm. Your 9 am has never been the sun's 9 am. It's mild — but it has been mild in the same direction every day of your life.

You were born at 1:00 pm on the clock. By the sun, it was 12:41 — you arrived at the top of the day, just past its peak.

Standing

The classroom the calendar built

// input: nz rolling school entry · first day = 5th birthday = tue 8 may 2007, mid-term 2

New Zealand does school entry like nowhere else: you start on your fifth birthday, whenever it falls. So you walked into a classroom for the first time on your birthday — Tuesday 8 May 2007, the middle of Term 2, the only new kid that day. Australia would have assigned you a cohort by decree; here, the school decided. And a May birthday is the deep middle of the ambiguity zone: some schools ran you through to Year 2 in 2008 — the young end of every room after that. Some held you the full year — the old end.

Only you know which one happened.

Then you were up to ten months younger than the top of your class for thirteen years. The literature is blunt about what that does: picked later, labelled less ready, outperformed by kids who were simply older — and it forks. Either effort stopped feeling connected to outcomes, or you built a real engine, because nothing arrived free. You'd know which. And you left school straight into 2020: your first year of adult life was a closed country.

Then you were the old end of every room — picked early, labelled able, confidence compounding on an eleven-month head start you didn't earn. The bill for that arrives in adulthood, the first time a field goes age-flat and the edge quietly isn't there. And your final school year was the COVID year — NCEA Level 3 through lockdowns, the leaver rituals cancelled or masked.

Forecast

What normal was set to

// input: calibration window, age 14–24 · may 2016 → may 2026 · closed three months ago

Fourteen to twenty-four is the window where your sense of "normal" gets set — what the world does, what it costs, whether it catches you. Yours: a mosque massacre at 16. A pandemic at 17. Your 18th birthday under Level 3, the takeaways freshly reopened. Borders shut until you were nearly 20. Then, at 20, the road home severed — Gabrielle closed the Brynderwyns and cut Northland off again. Someone ten years older calibrated on the John Key years: smooth, dull, upward. You calibrated on a country that could slam shut in 48 hours — and mostly caught you when it did.

The window closed on your 24th birthday, in May. Whatever normal is to you now, it's set.

You hold two defaults at once: high trust that collective action works — you watched five million people eliminate a virus — and low trust that access lasts. Roads, borders, shelves: you assume they can vanish, because yours did. Twice.

The interaction · rhythm × phase × standing × forecast

Steady ground, loud weather

Here is the shape of it. Your environment was the gentlest New Zealand offers — the flattest light curve in the country, a modest clock offset, the winterless north. Your era was the most disrupted decade in modern NZ history, and it landed precisely on your calibration window. So you're built to expect the background to hold and the shocks to arrive as events. You budget for disasters. You don't budget for seasons.

The blind spot that falls out of that: slow, cyclical erosion — the kind that never makes the news — is the one pattern your conditions never trained you to see coming.
