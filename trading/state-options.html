State

four treatments · nothing here is saved

The check-in is the same in all four — one pad, four corners, four facts, one optional line. The corners, the block on the gate, the lock once you have traded and the history coloured by what the ledger did are unchanged. Only the arrangement differs. Every one below is live: place a dot, tick things, resize the window.

First

The colour

this decides itself before any of the rest

Every option below marks the days behind you with what the ledger did — and it does that in green and red. That is the only judgement on this screen that is not self-reported, which is why it was worth colour. But a year of mornings is mostly red, so the pad greets you with a field of losses before you have done anything. Four ways to keep the fact and drop the alarm. Whichever you pick applies to every option on this page.

Note that this is the pad only. The ledger, the R figures and the calendar keep their green and red — there a colour is attached to one number you went looking for, not smeared across a background you are trying to think in front of. Say if you want those changed too and that is a separate job.

A

One column

no headings, no split

The two cards collapse into one. The pad leads, the sentence under it tells you where you landed, the four facts become a row you scan in one pass, and the note and the button share the last line. Nothing is titled, because a pad with four named corners does not need a label telling you it is a pad.

a green day a red day nothing traded

B

The pad is the screen

no card at all

No border, no panel, no button that looks like a button. The pad is large and sits on the glass directly; the facts are four quiet marks under it and the confirm is a line of type. The least form-like of the four — it reads as a thing you touch once rather than something you fill in.

C

The dial

round, like the backtesting ring

The same two axes bent into a circle. The centre is the middle of everything and the rim is the extreme, so how far out you place the dot is how strongly you feel it — a square gives you no way to say “a bit tilted”. It also rhymes with the ring on the backtesting screen, which is the only other round object in the app.

D

The band

one row, above everything else

The whole check-in as a single strip you could put at the top of any screen. Small pad, four ticks, done. It gives up the history behind you — the pad is too small to read it, and the outer labels with it — only the four corners survive, and the line beside the pad says where you landed. Fastest to answer, least to look at.

Round two

The ones that move

place a dot and watch

A to D differ only in arrangement. These four each add a behaviour — and three of them are behaviours that tell you something you cannot get from the picture standing still. Drag the dot around rather than tapping once; the effects are on the move, not on the load. All of them switch off if your machine asks for reduced motion, and nothing that moves is load-bearing.

E

The echo

what happened last time you were here

Place the dot and a ring goes out from it. Every past day it passes over lights up, and the line underneath counts them: how many days you have stood in this exact spot, and how they ended. This is the one that changes what the screen is for — the pad stops being a mood diary and becomes evidence. Drag slowly and watch the count change as you cross the field.

F

The trail

the week, drawn

Your last seven check-ins join up into a line that draws itself, faint at the oldest end and solid at today. One dot tells you where you are; a line tells you which way you have been going — and drifting from calm to agitated across four days is the thing you would never notice one morning at a time. The honest caveat: a week that genuinely jumps around draws a scribble rather than a drift. That is worth seeing too, but it is not as pretty as this one.

G

The breath

five and a half seconds a cycle

The mark you place breathes — two rings out and back on a slow cycle, the pace somebody would set for you if they were in the room. The only one here that is decoration, and it has a job: it holds you on the screen for a few seconds longer than you meant to stay, which is the entire point of checking in before you trade. Watch it for one cycle and you will find you have slowed down.

H

The weight

the corner fills with its own record

Whichever quadrant you are standing in fills, faintly, to how often days in that corner have ended green — a full corner is one that has worked, an almost empty one has not. It is a quantity, not a verdict: there is no colour and no number, only more or less of the same grey. Move between corners and you can feel the difference without being told off.

Round three

Different jobs

not a new look — a new thing to do

One and two both assumed the screen's job is record how you feel. These question that. I gives the check-in teeth; J says where you are matters less than how far it is from your normal; K adds a second moment in the evening that scores the morning; L connects one morning to the last; M throws the pad away entirely.

I

The limit

the check-in sets today's ceiling

The biggest idea on this page. Where you land sets a size and a count for the day, and the gate carries it: from Sharp, normal; from Tilted, one trade at half size. It turns a self-report into a constraint, which is the only way a self-report ever changes anything. The open question is whether it is advice or a rule — advice you will overrule on the exact morning it matters; a rule will make you hate it by March. Drag the dot and watch the line and the gate change.

J

The deviation

how far from your own normal

“Agitated” means nothing on its own — some people run agitated. What means something is agitated for you. A dashed crosshair marks the middle of your last thirty days and a line runs from it to where you are, so the reading is a distance rather than a position. Standing further out than you have all month is worth knowing even when the corner is a good one.

K

The evening

a second moment, that scores the morning

Everything else on this page records what you said. This is the only thing in the whole app that ever checks whether it was true. At the end of the day it puts your morning read beside what the ledger did and asks one question. Over weeks it produces the figure that actually matters: how often you can tell. Reading yourself correctly is a skill, and it is the only one here you can practise directly. Answer it below.

This morning, 07:41

Sharp — calm and charged

What the day did

Two trades — one green, one red, −0.4R

Reading yourself

7 of the last 10 calls were right

L

Yesterday

mornings that connect

Yesterday's dot stays on the pad, ringed, with what you wrote under it, before you place today's. Two mornings in a row saying the same thing is a fact about your life rather than about your morning — and you will only ever see that if the two are on screen together. The cheapest idea here and possibly the most useful.

Yesterday — Tilted “Still annoyed about Thursday. Told myself I would sit out and did not.”

M

One word

no pad at all

Throw the grid away. Eight words, pick one, done in a second — and a word is searchable and memorable in a way a coordinate is not. You will never think “I was at 0.7, 0.2”, but you will remember a run of Mondays that all said flat. What it gives up is the whole point of the pad: no distance, no drift, no history you can see at a glance. Fastest to answer, thinnest to look back on.

Round four

The field itself

not motion on a dot

Round two moved a thing — a ring, a line, a pulse. These change what the field is. Two of them draw the same data differently rather than decorating it, which is the only reason an effect earns the frame rate. Reload the page to see the ones that play on arrival; drag for the rest.

N

The sweep

the pad uncovers itself

A line goes round once and every past day fades in as it passes over, so the history arrives rather than being already there. It rhymes with the two radars on Metrics and the ring on Backtesting — this app already has a house style for round things that scan, and this is it applied to the pad. Costs one second, once, and then it is a normal pad forever.

O

Contours

the dots become the shape they make

Sixty scattered dots is sixty facts. The same sixty as contour lines is one fact: this is where you live. Tight rings mean most mornings land in one place; two separate hills mean there are two of you. The picture answers a question the dots only hold the evidence for — and it gets better, not messier, as the year fills up.

P

Focus

near is sharp, far goes soft

Days close to where you have put the dot are sharp; the rest fall out of focus. Your eye was going to do this anyway, so the pad may as well agree with it — and the effect is doing the counting for you: a spot with a dozen crisp days around it looks completely different from one with none. Drag slowly and the field pulls in and out.

Q

Parallax

the card tilts to you

Move the pointer over it and the whole thing tilts, with the days floating on a plane in front of the field so the two separate. Pure surface — it tells you nothing — but it is the one that makes the screen feel like an object rather than a form, and it costs nothing to carry. Works on a mouse; a phone gets the flat version.

R

The watermark

the corner says its own name

Cross into a corner and its name blooms huge behind the field, then settles back to almost nothing. You get told where you are without a label appearing anywhere, and because it fades rather than sticks it never becomes a sign you stop seeing. Drag between corners — it only fires on a change, so nudging around inside one corner stays quiet.
